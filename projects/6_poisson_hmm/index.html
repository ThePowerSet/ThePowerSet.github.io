Market Regimes with a Poisson HMM

A hidden Markov model for the daily number of S&P 500 stocks falling more than 5%, written from the likelihood up in R, with a count-based VaR.

On a median day, one stock in the panel falls more than 5%. On 16 March 2020, 368 of the 394 did. Counting those falls day by day gives a series that is quiet most of the time and violent in bursts, which is the shape a hidden Markov model is made for: an unseen regime sets how many stocks fall, and the regime moves according to a Markov chain. I built the model from the likelihood up, in R, used it to read twenty years of market stress and to forecast tomorrow's count.

Estimated transition matrix: the probability of tomorrow's state (columns) given today's (rows).

The chain forgets stress quickly. After a day in state 5, the next day is in state 1 or 2 with probability 50% (12% and 38%), and in state 5 again with probability 17%.

The algorithms, from scratch

The likelihood comes from the forward recursion: at each day the chain’s possible positions are propagated through \(\Gamma\) and then weighted by how well each state explains the count. Counts reach hundreds while the calm state has \(\lambda\) close to 1, so the emission probabilities underflow unless they are scaled; the scale factors then add up to the log-likelihood.

forward_pois <- function(delta, gamma, lambda, x) {
  T <- length(x); m <- length(delta)

  # log P(count | state) for every day and state; subtracting the row maximum
  # keeps exp() away from underflow when a count of 300 meets a state with lambda = 1
  logp  <- outer(x, lambda, function(x, l) dpois(x, l, log = TRUE))
  shift <- apply(logp, 1, max)
  p     <- exp(logp - shift)

  filt <- matrix(0, m, T)
  llik <- 0
  for (t in 1:T) {
    pred <- if (t == 1) delta else c(filt[, t - 1] %*% gamma)  # where can the chain be today?
    a    <- pred * p[t, ]                                      # and which state explains today's count?
    s    <- sum(a)
    llik <- llik + shift[t] + log(s)                           # the scale factors add up to the likelihood
    filt[, t] <- a / s
  }
  list(llik = llik, filtering = filt)
}

Baum–Welch alternates that with a backward pass that gives the smoothed probability of each state and of each move between states. Given those, the update is in closed form: each \(\lambda\) is a weighted average of the counts, and each row of \(\Gamma\) is the expected number of moves out of that state, split by destination.

# E_state[t, j]   = P(state j at day t | all data)         (smoothing, from the forward-backward pass)
# E_move[j, k, t] = P(state j at t, state k at t+1 | all data)
for (j in 1:m) {
  lambda[j] <- sum(x * E_state[, j]) / sum(E_state[, j])    # a weighted average of the counts
  for (k in 1:m)
    gamma[j, k] <- sum(E_move[j, k, ]) / sum(E_move[j, , ]) # expected j -> k moves over all moves out of j
}
delta <- stationary_dist(gamma)

I also maximised the likelihood directly with BFGS, on unconstrained working parameters (a multinomial logit for each row of \(\Gamma\)). It lands on the same log-likelihood as EM, −12,709.74, and on the same intensities to within 0.002. Standard errors come from the Hessian through the delta method: the highest intensity is 224.1 with a standard error of 2.5.

Does the model fit?

I checked it with forecast pseudo-residuals, which should look standard normal if the model is right. Their mean is 0.018 and their standard deviation 0.957; 2.5% of them fall beyond \(\pm 1.96\), against the 5% expected. The Viterbi path and the day-by-day (local) decoding disagree on 0.7% of days. Some tail misfit and residual autocorrelation remain: inside a state, the Poisson assumption does not capture all the dependence in the counts.

Forecasting tomorrow’s count

The same recursion gives a forecast. Propagate yesterday’s filtered state through \(\Gamma\) and the next count follows a mixture of Poissons, one per state. Its 95% and 99% quantiles are a VaR on counts: the number of stocks that should not be exceeded with that probability. It is a statement about breadth, not a monetary VaR.

To test it I refitted the model blind on the data up to 31 December 2025 (5,030 days) and forecast each of the 100 days to 27 May 2026 from the information available the day before. The busiest day of the window had 33 stocks down. The count rose above the 95% bound on 4 days and above the 99% bound on 0; with 100 days, 5 and 1 would be expected, so this is a sanity check rather than a test. The 99% bound is jumpy by construction: it moves into the distribution of state 5, around 200 stocks, whenever the predicted probability of that state exceeds 1%, which happened on 53 of the 100 days, and that is why it was never reached.
