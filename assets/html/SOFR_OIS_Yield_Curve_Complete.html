Executive Summary

This document provides a comprehensive guide to constructing SOFR (Secured Overnight Financing Rate) zero-coupon yield curves from market-observed OIS (Overnight Index Swap) swap rates. The project demonstrates a fundamental problem in quantitative finance: extracting the term structure of risk-free discount factors from observable market prices.

What is SOFR OIS?

SOFR is the successor to LIBOR, representing the virtually risk-free overnight funding rate in the United States. An OIS (Overnight Index Swap) is a floating/fixed rate swap where the floating leg compounds daily SOFR rates, while the fixed leg pays a fixed rate on the entire notional.

Key Insight: The market-quoted par rate for an OIS swap encodes information about the entire term structure of discount factors up to that maturity. By bootstrapping—solving sequentially for discount factors across different tenors—we can reconstruct the complete risk-free yield curve.

Why This Matters

  • Pricing: Discount factors are essential for valuing virtually all interest rate derivatives
  • Risk Management: Understanding the term structure allows accurate hedging and portfolio management
  • Post-LIBOR World: SOFR has become the standard risk-free reference rate, making OIS curves crucial for modern fixed income markets
  • Benchmark Rates: Central banks and market participants rely on these curves for policy and trading decisions

Financial Theory: Foundations

Discount Factors and Zero-Coupon Rates

The fundamental building block of fixed income markets is the discount factor—the present value of receiving $1 at a future date $t$:

$$DF(t) = \text{PV of } \$1 \text{ received at time } t$$

From a discount factor, we extract the continuously-compounded zero-coupon rate:

$$DF(t) = e^{-r_c(t) \cdot t}$$ $$r_c(t) = -\frac{\ln(DF(t))}{t}$$

The annual-compounded equivalent (often quoted in markets) is:

$$r_{\text{annual}}(t) = e^{r_c(t)} - 1$$

Interest Rate Swaps (IRS) Mechanics

An Interest Rate Swap (IRS) is a contract where two parties exchange interest payments:

  • Fixed leg: Receives a fixed rate $r_{\text{fixed}}$ on notional $N$
  • Floating leg: Pays a floating rate (in our case, compounded SOFR) on notional $N$

The Net Present Value (NPV) of an IRS is:

$$\text{NPV}_{\text{IRS}} = \sum_{i} \Delta t_i \cdot DF(t_i) \cdot (r_{\text{floating}, i} - r_{\text{fixed}}) + \text{notional} \cdot [DF(T_n) - DF(0)]$$

where the first term values the coupon payments and the second term accounts for the final notional exchange.

Par Swap Rates

A par swap rate is the fixed rate $r_{\text{par}}$ that makes the IRS NPV equal to zero:

$$\text{NPV}_{\text{IRS}}(r_{\text{par}}) = 0$$
Critical Observation: Given a par swap rate and all previously bootstrapped discount factors, the only unknown is the discount factor at that swap's maturity. We can solve for it using root-finding algorithms.

SOFR OIS Swaps

In an OIS swap, the floating leg compounds daily SOFR rates:

$$\text{Floating Accrual} = N \left[\prod_{d \in \text{days}} (1 + SOFR_d \times \frac{1}{360}) - 1\right]$$

This daily compounding reduces credit and duration risks compared to standard interest rate swaps. Since SOFR is virtually risk-free, OIS swaps serve as the foundation for the risk-free yield curve.

Mathematical Methods: Bootstrapping & Interpolation

Log-Linear Interpolation of Discount Factors

Once we have bootstrapped discount factors at discrete maturities, we need a continuous function to interpolate discount factors at intermediate dates. The chosen method is log-linear interpolation.

Between two nodes $(t_1, DF_1)$ and $(t_2, DF_2)$, we interpolate the logarithm of discount factors linearly:

$$\ln(DF(t)) = \ln(DF_1) + \frac{t - t_1}{t_2 - t_1} \left[\ln(DF_2) - \ln(DF_1)\right]$$

Inverting this:

$$DF(t) = DF_1^{\frac{t_2 - t}{t_2 - t_1}} \cdot DF_2^{\frac{t - t_1}{t_2 - t_1}}$$

Equivalence to Linear Zero Rate Interpolation

A remarkable property: log-linear interpolation of discount factors is mathematically equivalent to linear interpolation of continuously-compounded zero rates.

Starting from $\ln(DF(t)) = -r_c(t) \cdot t$, the interpolation becomes:

$$-r_c(t) \cdot t = -r_1 \cdot t_1 + \frac{t - t_1}{t_2 - t_1}(r_2 \cdot t_2 - r_1 \cdot t_1)$$

Solving for $r_c(t)$:

$$r_c(t) = r_1 + \frac{t - t_1}{t_2 - t_1}(r_2 - r_1)$$
Key Result: $$\boxed{\text{Log-linear interpolation of DFs} \iff \text{Linear interpolation of zero rates}}$$ This ensures smooth, economically sensible curves with no arbitrage opportunities between interpolation nodes.

Bootstrapping Algorithm: Sequential Root-Finding

Bootstrapping solves for discount factors sequentially. At each tenor $T_n$:

Bootstrapping Step:
  1. We know: par swap rate $r_n$ and all previous discount factors $\{DF(T_1), \ldots, DF(T_{n-1})\}$
  2. Unknown: $DF(T_n)$
  3. Equation: $\text{NPV}(\text{IRS}(r_n, T_n)) = 0$ where $\text{NPV}$ depends on $DF(T_n)$
  4. Root-finding: Solve for $DF(T_n)$ that satisfies the equation
  5. Add to curve: $\{DF(T_1), \ldots, DF(T_n)\}$ and interpolate forward

Bisection Algorithm for Root-Finding

The bisection algorithm is a robust, derivative-free method for finding roots of a continuous function. Given a sign-changing bracket $[a, b]$ where $f(a) \cdot f(b) < 0$:

Bisection Loop:
  1. Compute midpoint: $m = \frac{a + b}{2}$
  2. Evaluate: $f(m)$
  3. Update bracket:
    • If $f(a) \cdot f(m) < 0$: set $b \leftarrow m$ (root is in $[a, m]$)
    • Else: set $a \leftarrow m$ (root is in $[m, b]$)
  4. Repeat until $|f(m)| < \epsilon$ (typically $\epsilon = 10^{-10}$)

Properties:

  • Guaranteed convergence (if bracket found)
  • Linear convergence rate: error halves each iteration → ~33 iterations for $10^{-10}$ accuracy
  • No derivative required → robust for non-smooth functions
  • Why it works for IRS: NPV is monotonic in the terminal discount factor

Method 1: Using rateslib Built-in Solver

rateslib is a Python library for interest rate derivatives valuation and curve construction. It provides a high-level Solver class that automatically handles bootstrapping.

Overview

Aspect Details

User Role Define curve structure, instruments, and target rates; let solver find DFs
Efficiency Fast, typically converges in seconds for full curve
Transparency Less educational; harder to debug intermediate steps
Robustness Highly robust for well-behaved market data

Step 1: Load Data and Set Dates

import pandas as pd
from rateslib import dt, add_tenor
from rateslib.curves import Curve
from rateslib.instruments import IRS
from rateslib.solver import Solver

# Load consolidated SOFR panel
panel_long = pd.read_csv("SOFR_panel_Date_Tenor_Rate_sorted.csv", parse_dates=["Date"])

# Extract data for a specific date
data = sofr_curve("2025-11-14")

# Set base date and settlement date (T+2 business days, US convention)
base_date = dt(2025, 11, 14)
settle_date = add_tenor(base_date, "2B", "MF", "nyc")

# Compute termination dates for each tenor
data["Termination"] = [add_tenor(settle_date, t, "F", "nyc") for t in data["Term"]]

Step 2: Create Curve Object

Initialize an empty curve with node placeholders. The solver will update these nodes:

sofr = Curve(
    id="sofr",
    convention="Act360",           # Day count: Actual/360
    calendar="nyc",                # Holiday calendar: New York
    modifier="MF",                 # Modified Following convention
    interpolation="log_linear",    # Log-linear DF interpolation
    nodes={
        base_date: 1.0,           # Trade date DF = 1
        **{d: 1.0 for d in data["Termination"]},  # Placeholder nodes
    },
)

Step 3: Define Instruments and Solver

Create IRS instruments for each tenor and instantiate the solver:

solver = Solver(
    curves=[sofr],
    instruments=[IRS(settle_date, d, spec="usd_irs", curves="sofr") 
                 for d in data["Termination"]],
    s=data["Rate"],                        # Target rates (market par rates)
    instrument_labels=data["Term"],        # Labels for identification
    id="sofr_curve",
)
# Solver automatically bootstraps all discount factors

Step 4: Extract Results

import numpy as np

# Compute discount factors and zero rates
data["DF"] = [float(sofr[d]) for d in data["Termination"]]
data["T_years"] = (data["Termination"] - base_date).dt.days / 365.0

# Continuously-compounded zero rate
data["Zero_cc"] = -np.log(data["DF"]) / data["T_years"]

# Annual-compounded zero rate
data["Zero_annual"] = np.exp(data["Zero_cc"]) - 1.0

# Display results
print(data[["Term", "DF", "Zero_cc", "Zero_annual"]])

Implementation: Step-by-Step

Step 1: Data Preparation (Same as Method 1)

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from rateslib import dt, add_tenor
from rateslib.curves import Curve
from rateslib.instruments import IRS

# Load data
panel_long = pd.read_csv("SOFR_panel_Date_Tenor_Rate_sorted.csv", parse_dates=["Date"])

# Select date and compute dates
trade_date = dt(2025, 11, 14)
settle_date = add_tenor(trade_date, "2B", "MF", "nyc")

data = sofr_curve("2025-11-14")
data["Termination"] = [add_tenor(settle_date, t, "MF", "nyc") for t in data["Tenor"]]

Step 2: Curve Builder Function

Create a utility function to build curves from discount factor nodes:

def build_curve_from_nodes(nodes_dict):
    """
    Build a Curve object from a dictionary of {date: DF} pairs.
    
    The curve uses log-linear interpolation of discount factors,
    which is equivalent to linear interpolation of zero rates.
    """
    return Curve(
        id="sofr_boot",
        convention="Act360",
        calendar="nyc",
        modifier="MF",
        interpolation="log_linear",
        nodes=nodes_dict,
    )

Step 3: Bisection Solver Function

The core algorithm: solve for DF(T) such that IRS NPV = 0:

def solve_df_for_irs(tenor, termination_date, market_rate, known_nodes):
    """
    Bisection algorithm to find DF(termination_date) such that 
    the IRS with fixed rate = market_rate has NPV = 0.
    
    Parameters:
    - tenor: Tenor label (e.g., "5Y")
    - termination_date: End date of swap
    - market_rate: Par swap rate (market quote)
    - known_nodes: Dict of {date: DF} for all previously solved maturities
    
    Returns:
    - DF(termination_date) that zeros the IRS NPV
    """
    
    # Define the IRS instrument
    irs = IRS(
        effective=settle_date,
        termination=termination_date,
        fixed_rate=market_rate,
        spec="usd_irs",
        curves="sofr_boot",
    )
    
    # Define the NPV function as a function of DF(T)
    def npv_given_df(df_T):
        # Rebuild curve with new terminal DF
        nodes = {trade_date: 1.0, **known_nodes, termination_date: df_T}
        curve = build_curve_from_nodes(nodes)
        # Return NPV
        return float(irs.npv(curves=curve))
    
    # === BRACKET SELECTION ===
    low, high = 0.1, 0.8
    f_low = npv_given_df(low)
    f_high = npv_given_df(high)
    
    # Expand bracket if signs don't change
    for _ in range(5):
        if f_low * f_high < 0:  # Sign change found
            break
        low *= 0.5
        high *= 1.5
        f_low = npv_given_df(low)
        f_high = npv_given_df(high)
    
    if f_low * f_high > 0:
        raise RuntimeError(
            f"Could not find a valid bracket for tenor {tenor}. "
            f"Tried [0.1, 0.8] and expansions."
        )
    
    # === BISECTION LOOP ===
    for iteration in range(60):  # Usually converges in ~30 iterations
        mid = 0.5 * (low + high)
        f_mid = npv_given_df(mid)
        
        # Convergence check
        if abs(f_mid) < 1e-10:
            return mid
        
        # Update bracket
        if f_low * f_mid < 0:
            # Root is in [low, mid]
            high, f_high = mid, f_mid
        else:
            # Root is in [mid, high]
            low, f_low = mid, f_mid
    
    return mid  # Final estimate

Step 4: Bootstrapping Loop

Sequentially solve for discount factors across all tenors:

nodes = {trade_date: 1.0}   # Initialize with trade date DF = 1
df_rows = []

for _, row in data.iterrows():
    tenor = row["Tenor"]
    rate = row["Rate"]
    termination = row["Termination"]
    
    # Solve for this tenor's DF
    df_T = solve_df_for_irs(tenor, termination, rate, nodes)
    
    # Add to known nodes for next iteration
    nodes[termination] = df_T
    
    # Compute zero rates
    T_years = (termination - trade_date).days / 360  # Act/360 convention
    zero_cc = -np.log(df_T) / T_years
    zero_annual = np.exp(zero_cc) - 1.0
    
    # Store results
    df_rows.append({
        "Tenor": tenor,
        "Termination": termination,
        "Par_rate_%": rate,
        "DF": df_T,
        "Zero_cc": zero_cc,
        "Zero_annual": zero_annual,
    })

# Convert to DataFrame
df_curve = pd.DataFrame(df_rows)
print(df_curve)

Visualization: NPV Function and Bisection Bracket

The bisection algorithm works because the IRS NPV function is monotonic in the terminal discount factor. Here's how to visualize this: 

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Select a tenor for visualization
idx = data.index[data["Tenor"] == "5Y"][0]
inst = sanity_inst[idx]
end_date = data["Termination"].iloc[idx]

# Define NPV function and compute curve
def irs_npv_given_df(df_value):
    original_df = float(sofr._nodes.nodes[end_date])
    sofr.update_node(end_date, df_value)
    try:
        npv = float(inst.npv(curves=sofr))
    finally:
        sofr.update_node(end_date, original_df)
    return npv

# Compute NPV across a grid
df_star = data["DF"].iloc[idx]
df_grid = np.linspace(df_star * 0.9, df_star * 1.1, 200)
npv_grid = np.array([irs_npv_given_df(df) for df in df_grid])

# Plot
plt.figure(figsize=(10, 6))
plt.plot(df_grid, npv_grid, linewidth=2, label="NPV(DF)")
plt.axhline(0, linestyle="--", color="black", alpha=0.5)
plt.axvline(df_star, linestyle="--", color="red", label=f"DF* = {df_star:.6f}")
plt.fill_between(df_grid, npv_grid, 0, where=(npv_grid > 0), 
                 color="green", alpha=0.3, label="NPV > 0")
plt.fill_between(df_grid, npv_grid, 0, where=(npv_grid < 0),
                 color="red", alpha=0.3, label="NPV < 0")
plt.xlabel("Discount Factor")
plt.ylabel("Net Present Value ($)")
plt.title("IRS NPV as a Function of DF(5Y) — Bisection finds the root")
plt.legend()
plt.grid(True, alpha=0.3)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Practical Applications

Pricing Interest Rate Derivatives

Once the curve is bootstrapped, it serves as the discount curve for any fixed income instrument:

Example: Price a 3.5Y OIS swap with 3.00% fixed rate

  1. Interpolate DF(3.5Y): Use log-linear interpolation between 3Y [DF=0.9056] and 4Y [DF=0.8845] nodes → DF(3.5Y) ≈ 0.8948
  2. Discount fixed coupons: $1M × 3% × DF(3.5Y) × (payment frequency factor)
  3. Discount floating coupons: Compound daily SOFR using bootstrap curve for each sub-period
  4. Compute NPV: PV(fixed) - PV(floating) = NPV relative to 3% par rate

Risk Management & Hedging

From the bootstrapped curve, key risk metrics are derived:

  • DV01 (Dollar Value of 1 bps): Sensitivity of portfolio value to 1 bp parallel shift in the curve. For a $100M 5Y IRS, DV01 ≈ $400-500 (depending on contract specifics)
  • Key Rate Durations: Sensitivity to curve shifts at specific tenors (e.g., "2Y Key Rate Duration") — enables "bucketing" of risk
  • Curve Trades: Long curve / short curve, butterfly spreads, etc., based on expected curve shape changes
  • Hedge Ratios: How many units of liquid benchmark swaps (e.g., 2Y, 5Y, 10Y) needed to neutralize directional risk

Model Calibration

The bootstrapped curve serves as input to more complex models:

  • Short-rate models (Vasicek, CIR, Hull-White): Calibrate volatility and mean reversion to reproduce the bootstrapped curve
  • Forward rate models (HJM): Use curve and volatility surface to price exotic derivatives
  • Multi-curve frameworks: Combine SOFR curve with basis curves (3M SOFR vs SOFR) for accurate cross-currency pricing

To ensure the bootstrapped curve is accurate, several validation checks are performed:

Technical Details & Conventions

Day Count Convention

The project uses Actual/360 (Act/360) convention, which is standard for SOFR-based instruments:

$$\text{Accrual} = N \times r \times \frac{\text{actual days}}{360}$$

For example, a 6-month (181 days) SOFR rate of 3.80%:

$$\text{Accrual} = \$1,000,000 \times 0.038 \times \frac{181}{360} = \$19,117$$

Calendar Convention

All dates are adjusted using New York (NYSE) business day conventions:

  • Calendar: NYSE holidays (Christmas, Thanksgiving, etc.)
  • Roll convention: Modified Following (MF) — if date falls on weekend/holiday, roll forward to next business day, but if that crosses month boundary, roll back

Settlement Convention

T+2 (Trade plus 2 business days) — standard for USD interest rate swaps. For a trade date of 2025-11-14 (Friday):

  • 2025-11-14: Trade date
  • 2025-11-17: Monday (skip weekend)
  • 2025-11-18: Tuesday (T+2 settlement)

Tenor Notation

Tenors follow the convention:

  • nW: n weeks (1W, 2W, 3W)
  • nM: n months (1M, 2M, ..., 35M)
  • nY: n years (3Y, 4Y, ..., 50Y)
