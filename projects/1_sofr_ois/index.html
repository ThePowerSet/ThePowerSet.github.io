SOFR OIS Yield Curve Construction

From 86 swap quotes to a USD discount curve, solved with a library and with a bisection I wrote.

A swap quote is a price. Each SOFR OIS rate, from one week to fifty years, is the fixed rate that makes a swap worth as much as overnight SOFR compounded over the same stretch, and 86 of them are enough to solve for the discount factor at every one of those maturities. This page follows the path from quotes to curve: how a discount factor falls out of a quote, two ways of computing it, and what the curve looks like at the end.

A quote pins down one discount factor

Take the textbook case, a swap that starts today. The floating leg, overnight SOFR compounded, is worth \(1 - DF(T_n)\). The fixed leg pays the rate \(S_n\) times the year fraction \(\alpha_i\) on each payment date. Setting the two equal gives the par rate:

\[S_n = \frac{1 - DF(T_n)}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i \, DF(T_i)}\]

If the discount factors of the earlier payment dates are already known, the only unknown on the right is \(DF(T_n)\). One quote, one unknown, so the curve can be built from the short end outwards: solve the one-week swap, then two weeks, and so on to fifty years, adding one node each time.

The real swaps are less tidy. They start two business days after the trade date, follow a New York calendar and pay two business days after each period ends, so rateslib prices the actual swaps and my code only has to find the node.

The NPV crosses zero once, at DF(5Y) = 0.84288, and that is the number the bootstrap is after. It decreases as the discount factor grows, so a bracket whose ends have opposite signs holds exactly one root, and the half where the sign flips still holds it. Bisection needs nothing else. The line is nearly straight, so a secant iteration would get there in about 7 NPV evaluations instead of 54 (medians over the 86 nodes); bisection trades those evaluations for needing nothing but the sign.

market(day) returns that day’s quotes and swap dates, read from the table I put together out of the 86 BlueGamma files.

def solve_df(swap, known, end):
    """DF at `end` that makes `swap` price at par, given the discount factors before it."""

    def npv(df):
        # rebuild the curve with a trial value at the new node and price the swap on it
        return float(swap.npv(curves=make_curve({**known, end: df})))

    # DF is close to 1 for a one-week swap and about 0.17 at 50Y: start wide, widen until the sign flips
    lo, hi = 0.1, 0.8
    f_lo, f_hi = npv(lo), npv(hi)
    while f_lo * f_hi > 0:
        lo, hi = lo / 2, hi * 1.5
        f_lo, f_hi = npv(lo), npv(hi)

    for _ in range(60):
        mid = 0.5 * (lo + hi)
        f_mid = npv(mid)
        if abs(f_mid) < 1e-10:      # NPV is zero to within 1e-10 dollars
            break
        if f_lo * f_mid < 0:        # root is in the lower half
            hi = mid
        else:
            lo, f_lo = mid, f_mid
    return mid


def bootstrap(day):
    trade, settle, tenors, rates, ends = market(day)
    nodes = {trade: 1.0}                          # today a dollar is worth a dollar
    for rate, end in zip(rates, ends):            # shortest swap first
        swap = IRS(settle, end, fixed_rate=rate, spec="usd_irs", curves="sofr")
        nodes[end] = solve_df(swap, nodes, end)
    return nodes

Two routes, same curve

Both curves reprice all 86 quotes. The largest miss is 0.00009 bp for the bisection and 0.000001 bp for the solver, and the discount factors differ by at most \(5 \cdot 10^{-8}\), at the 19-year node.

Where that last difference comes from

The bisection goes one swap at a time, so when it solves the 5-year node nothing exists beyond it. That swap’s last payment falls two business days after the node (20 November 2030 against 18 November), past the last known point, so the discount factor there is extrapolated. The solver fits all nodes at once and interpolates it instead. A second bisection pass, with all the other nodes in place, closes the gap to \(3 \cdot 10^{-9}\).

Par quotes, zero rates and forward rates on 14 November 2025. Zero and forward rates are Act/360 and annually compounded, so from one year out they sit on the same footing as the annual-pay quotes. Hover for values; the buttons change the maturity axis.

The zero rate falls from 4.06% at one week to 3.35% at three years, climbs to 4.15% at 22 years and comes back to 3.58% at fifty. The forwards turn earlier, because the zero rate is their running average: they peak at 4.63% around 15 years, are down to 3.55% by 30 and end at 1.91%. Up to 30 years the zero rate stays within about 10 bp of the par quotes; at 50 years it is 22 bp below them.

What log-linear interpolation does

Between nodes I interpolate the discount factor log-linearly. That holds the forward rate constant on each interval, which is the staircase in the chart, and it makes the zero rate the average of the forwards so far, not a straight line between nodes. With a zero rate of 2% at one year and 3% at two, log-linear interpolation gives 2.67% at eighteen months, where a straight line in zero rates would give 2.5%.

On 14 November all 86 discount factors are positive and decreasing and every forward is non-negative, which is what a curve needs to be free of static arbitrage. The forwards jump at each node. A smoother scheme, such as monotone convex, would remove the jumps.

Twenty days, same function

The pipeline is a function of the date, so I ran it on each of the 20 days of quotes.
