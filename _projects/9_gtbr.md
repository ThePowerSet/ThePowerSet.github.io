<p class="proj-lead">
A delta-hedged option earns from curvature and pays for time. The gamma P&L grows with the square of the day's move, theta is a fixed daily bill, and the two cancel at one particular move: \(\sigma/\sqrt{365}\) for an at-the-money option. I derived that number and asked a simple question: if the dealers who sold those options hedge in the direction of a move that large, does the market keep going once it passes the line? I tested the rule on 33 years of SPY with the VIX as the volatility input. On this evidence the answer is no.

## Where gamma and theta cancel

Hold a long at-the-money option and delta-hedge it every day. Over one day its P&L is approximately $$\tfrac12\,\Gamma\rho^2 + \Theta$$, where $$\rho$$ is the relative move of the underlying, $$\Gamma$$ the dollar gamma and $$\Theta$$ the theta of one day. Black–Scholes gives

$$\Gamma = \frac{S\,N'(d_1)}{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}}, \qquad \Theta_{year} = -\frac{\sigma\,S\,N'(d_1)}{2\sqrt{\tau}}$$

once the interest-rate term is dropped, and the theta of one calendar day is a 365th of the yearly one, $$\Theta_{day} = \Theta_{year}/365$$. Setting the P&L to zero:

$$\rho^2 = -\frac{2\,\Theta_{day}}{\Gamma} = \frac{\sigma^2}{365}$$

In the ratio $$N'(d_1)$$ and the maturity cancel, so the break-even move depends on volatility alone: if the underlying moves by what the implied volatility says it should, the holder of the option breaks even. It is a break-even for P&L; nothing forces anyone to act at that level. Counted in trading days instead of calendar days it becomes $$\sigma/\sqrt{252}$$, 20% wider, and that is the version the rule below uses.

```python
def delta_hedged_pnl(rho, S, sigma, tau, days_per_year=365):
    """One day of P&L for a delta-hedged long ATM option, when the underlying moves by rho."""
    d1 = 0.5 * sigma * np.sqrt(tau)                  # ATM, no interest rate
    n = norm.pdf(d1)
    dollar_gamma = S * n / (sigma * np.sqrt(tau))    # curvature, in dollars
    theta_day = -sigma * S * n / (2 * np.sqrt(tau)) / days_per_year   # the daily bill
    return 0.5 * dollar_gamma * rho**2 + theta_day
```

## From a break-even to a rule

The dealer who sold the option is on the other side of this chart: past the range, every extra point of move costs more than the theta collected. Hedging by buying rallies and selling dips, such a dealer can push the market further in the direction of the move. That holds only if the dealers' net gamma is negative, which cannot be observed from price data, so the rule is a bet on the hypothesis: when the day's move passes the GTBR, it continues.

The threshold for each day comes from the previous close of the VIX, so nothing from the future leaks in. The VIX is a model-free 30-day estimate of variance from out-of-the-money SPX options, not an at-the-money implied volatility; I use it as the volatility input anyway. My first version trades the first break on hourly bars, with a stop at one GTBR, a target at two, an exit at the close and costs of 1 bp plus 1 bp of slippage per side. Free hourly data stops at two years, so to test the same signal on a long sample I wrote a daily proxy: enter at the threshold level in the direction of the break, exit at the close.

```python
def build_trades(d):
    up = d["open"] * np.exp(+d["gtbr"])        # the level that, if touched, says "the move is bigger than implied"
    dn = d["open"] * np.exp(-d["gtbr"])
    hit_up, hit_dn = d["high"] >= up, d["low"] <= dn
    both = hit_up & hit_dn                     # both touched: the order inside the day is unknown, so skip the day
    cost = 2 * (COST_BPS + SLIPPAGE_BPS) / 1e4 # round trip, in log-return terms

    t = pd.DataFrame(index=d.index)
    t["direction"] = np.where(hit_up & ~both, 1, np.where(hit_dn & ~both, -1, 0))
    t["entry"] = np.where(t["direction"] == 1, up, dn)             # filled at the level, in the direction of the break
    t["ret"] = t["direction"] * np.log(d["close"] / t["entry"]) - cost   # held to the close
    return t[t["direction"] != 0], int(both.sum())
```

Days that touch both levels are skipped, since the order inside the day is unknown: 25 of 8,396.

## Two years of hourly bars

The hourly version, with stop, target and the time filters, produced 36 trades between June 2024 and June 2026. They returned +0.017% per trade after costs, a profit factor of 1.05 and a daily Sharpe of 0.07; the bootstrap gives $$p = 0.46$$ and the 95% interval for the return per trade runs from −0.29% to +0.34%, so this sample says nothing either way. 35 of the 36 trades ended at the close, so the stop and the target barely mattered. ## What would change the answer

The missing ingredient is the one the hypothesis rests on: the sign of the dealers' gamma. Positioning data (GEX), a calendar of macro announcements, which produce breaks that are scheduled rather than driven by hedging, and a longer intraday history are the next steps, along with a variant that buys a straddle at the break instead of trading its direction. The break-even itself stands as a result of the option maths; what the tests say is that, on SPY and as a continuation signal, it does not pay for its costs.
